Classic scene: the timer goes off, you open the door, sponge stands tall and golden. A minute later — a pit in the middle and a rippled crust. Let's unpack where that comes from.

## 1. You opened the door too early

A sponge holds its shape thanks to hot air expanding the bubbles inside. Opening the oven before minute 25 pulls cold air in and the foam collapses instantly.

**What to do:** don't open the oven for the first 25-30 minutes. At all. Watch through the glass, use your phone flashlight — anything but opening the door.

## 2. You cut the heat too abruptly

Pulling the sponge out of the heat when it's hot and fragile shocks the structure. It needs a gradual step-down.

**What to do:** turn off the oven, crack the door 5-7 cm, leave the sponge inside for another 10 minutes. Then take it out. Not an extra step — it's your insurance against sinking.

## 3. Too much liquid in the batter

A bento cake bakes in a small tin — 14-16 cm. Small tin → tall column of batter → more weight bearing down on the lower layers. If the batter is thin, it won't set fast enough before the centre gives under its own weight.

**What to do:** stick to the ratios to the gram. Not “roughly.” This especially matters for water, milk and melted butter — even 15 extra grams of fat turns an airy sponge into a pancake. Exact gram weights are in the PDF collection.

## 4. Under-whipped egg white

If you whip the whites with sugar to soft peaks, they release their air during baking. You need stiff peaks — the tip stands straight and doesn't curl.

**What to do:** whip whites in a clean, dry bowl (zero grease residue!), add sugar gradually, take it to a glossy stiff peak. Three to four minutes on a decent hand mixer.

## 5. You didn't let the sponge rest

A fresh, hot sponge is moist and fragile at the same time. Crumb-coating it straight from the oven almost guarantees deformation.

**What to do:** cool the sponge to room temperature, wrap it in plastic, refrigerate for 6-12 hours. The moisture redistributes, the structure settles, the texture becomes elastic. Then you cut and assemble — and the cake holds its shape to the last slice.
