The sponge rose in the oven and looked perfect — then five minutes on the counter and it sank and turned dense. It's the most common complaint from beginners, and the cause is always the same in nature.

## What holds a sponge up

While the batter is in the oven, its height is held by **steam and the air in the bubbles**. Egg white, starch and flour are meanwhile building the frame that has to lock that height in place.

If the frame sets in time, the sponge stays tall after cooling too. If it doesn't, the steam escapes, the bubbles deflate, and the structure folds under its own weight. Sinking always means **the frame didn't keep up with the gas**.

## Where it gets decided

There aren't many reasons the frame falls behind, and all of them are a matter of technique rather than recipe:

- the ratio of liquid to flour in the batter;
- how the egg white was folded in, and whether it lost volume in the process;
- temperature and time in the oven;
- how the sponge was cooled, and whether it was given time to settle before assembly.

Each of these has a specific right value — and it differs for a chocolate, poppy seed, pistachio or coconut sponge. There is no single universal number here, which is exactly why generic advice off the internet often doesn't work.

## Why it hurts more on a bento

A bento cake has only 2–3 layers across 6–8 cm of height. A sunken sponge isn't "slightly lower" — it's ruined proportions: the cream starts to dominate and the cut looks heavy.

And on a small diameter the error shows more: in a 10 cm tin the structure holds worse than in a 20 cm one, because there is less frame to hold it.
