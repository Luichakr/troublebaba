Two similar failures get confused. Cream that **slumped** came together first and then lost its shape. Cream that **won't thicken** never came together at all: it stays liquid however long you whip it. The causes are different.

## What is happening physically

Cream thickens not from time under the whisk but because fat builds a structure that holds moisture. If there isn't enough fat, or the fat is warm, there is nothing for that structure to form from — and extra minutes on the mixer don't help, they hurt: over-whipped cream splits.

Hence the main point: if the cream hasn't begun to thicken within a reasonable time, continuing is pointless. It only gets worse from there.

## Three places the fat is lost

**Cream fat content.** Cream below a certain percentage will not whip at all — that is physics, not technique. Vegetable cream behaves differently from dairy, and not always predictably.

**Temperature.** Warm fat holds no structure. That is why every recipe starts by saying the ingredients must be cold — not for convenience, but because otherwise the cream will not come together.

**Ratios by eye.** A cream is a proportion. One spare spoon of liquid, or a shortfall of dry ingredients, changes the result more than it seems, and fixing a finished cream by adding things is almost always futile.

## Why this is expensive

A batch of cream for a bento is a tub of cheese, plus butter or cream. A ruined batch is not recoverable: split cream does not come back together, and "rescuing" it with additions gives you a cream you cannot trust with an order.

This is why bakers working on orders don't arrive at a cream recipe by experiment. They take a proven formula and repeat it exactly — cheaper than any experiment.

If the cream came together first and slumped afterwards, that is a different story: [cream won't hold its shape](/en/blog/cream-not-holding-shape/).
